# The seabed and the water

How the ocean floor and the underwater look are built, what exists today, and the plan to reach the owner's vision. The vision itself is the owner's private reference, `docs/THALASSA_Seabed_Environment_Reference.md`, which is not in git. In one line: **a seabed that feels naturally varied at every scale, shaped by geology, mostly natural, sometimes alien, often empty, so that the rare enormous formation makes even Thalassa feel very small.**

## What exists today (2026-11-08)

- **Terrain:** `Content/World/Seabed/SM_Seabed`, generated by `Scripts/blender/make_seabed.py` and imported by `Scripts/unreal/import_seabed.py`. It covers 6 × 6 km at 10 m resolution, drawn with Nanite, and its collision is the terrain itself. It has a basin 500 m down under the start, ridges to about 280 m, a winding canyon to starboard down to 653 m, a seamount ahead to port with its peak 101 m below the surface, and a gentle rise at the edges. It is deterministic: the same script always makes the same seabed, so the sonar always charts the same thing.
- **Material:** `M_Seabed`. Sand on the flats and rock on the slopes, from vertex colours, broken up by two scales of world-space noise. A placeholder.
- **Water:** in `Scripts/unreal/create_test_map.py`, tuned by the constants at its top:
  - dense, even murk at every depth, with volumetric scattering so lamps cast beams;
  - dim, cold light from above, and an ambient light whose lower half is black;
  - fixed exposure, so dark water stays dark instead of the camera brightening it;
  - a cold grade, a vignette, light grain and slight colour fringing.
- **Floodlights:** five spotlights on the hull (`Floodlights` in `BP_Thalassa`). Two at the bow light the water ahead of the bridge window, and three under the keel point at the seabed. They cast no shadows. Move or retune them in the Blueprint.
- **Marine snow:** `AThalassaMarineSnow`, 1,200 specks around each player's camera. They're fixed in the water, drifting and sinking slowly, so they stream past when the ship moves. They're lit like everything else, so they mostly show in the lamp beams, and they're hidden inside the ship's dry compartments.
- **First formations (2026-11-08):** `Scripts/blender/make_formations.py` makes one family and a landmark, imported by `Scripts/unreal/import_formations.py` into `Content/World/Formations`. There are six slab towers (15 to 24 m tall), five broken slab fragments, and `SM_Monolith_Bent`: about 300 m tall, bending as it rises, with strata of varying thickness that dip across it, worn-back soft layers, vertical fractures, and rust and pale mineral streaks. They're Nanite, with collision as their real shape, and use the seabed material for their vertex colours.
- **Placement by rules** (in `create_test_map.py`, same result every time):
  - **The monolith** stands alone on the flat basin floor at (-500, -420) m, 650 m astern of the start and clear of every test route, with five huge fallen blocks of its broken top at its foot, on the side it leans toward.
  - **The slab towers,** fourteen of them, stand only on moderate slopes (12 to 60%) in the south-west, at least 70 m apart and away from the map's rim. Each has 5 to 11 broken plates scattered around its feet.
  - **Sinking:** every piece is sunk to the lowest ground under its footprint, so nothing floats.
- **Not there yet:** surface detail. Geometry alone gives either facets or clay: the plates' ribbed edges help at a distance, but close up rock needs scanned textures (Megascans, below). Also missing: more formation families, regions, a water surface, sediment clouds, sound.

## The hard technical truths

1. **A heightmap can't make most of the vision.** Arches, overhangs, caves, mushroom rocks, stacked slabs and cathedral caverns all fold back over themselves, and a heightfield can't. The terrain stays a heightfield for the broad shape (plains, basins, canyons, seamounts, escarpments). Everything vertical or hollow is **meshes placed on it**: Nanite static meshes, any number of triangles, sunk into the ground so the joins don't show.
2. **Placement must follow rules, not randomness.** The reference asks for formation families, consistent orientation, a few big pieces with many small ones, and empty space. Unreal's built-in **PCG framework** (free, in the engine) is made for this. Rules run on the terrain ("on slopes over 35°, place a cliff family; below it, a scree of angular boulders aimed downhill"), and the results are baked into the level.
3. **Bake, don't generate at runtime.** Every player must see, collide with and sonar-map the same seabed. Generating at edit time and saving the result guarantees that, and costs nothing in multiplayer.
4. **The hull test must stay cheap.** Each tick, the ship sweeps its hull against the world. Thousands of rocks with full triangle collision would make that expensive. Small debris gets no collision. Formations get simple collision shapes. Only big walls and arches get their real shape.
5. **The sonar needs to see formations.** Its cells are 25 m, so a 10 m pillar is invisible to it today. Finer local resolution (1 to 2 m near the ship) is already on the sonar's list, and it matters more once formations exist. See the notes below on sonar and materials.
6. **World size.** 6 km is a test region. The real ocean needs World Partition, Unreal's streaming of big worlds in cells, and the seabed in tiles. The generator already works per region, so it can make tiles.

## Assets: what's free

- **Quixel Megascans (now on Fab).** Thousands of scanned rocks, cliffs, sand, gravel and shells: exactly the "rocks and boulders", "debris" and "materials" sections. Megascans moved from Quixel Bridge to Fab in late 2024, and the terms changed then: some assets became free for Unreal Engine use, and others need a licence. **Check each asset's licence on Fab before using it**, and record it in `docs/ASSET_LICENSES.md`, as for every third-party asset. Fab is opened from inside the Unreal editor (the Fab button in the toolbar), signed in with the owner's Epic account. Claude can't sign in or accept terms for the owner.
- **Fab's free monthly assets.** Epic gives away a few each month, sometimes rock and nature packs. Claimed ones stay free.
- **Epic's sample projects.** Electric Dreams and others include rock and nature assets with PCG examples, free for Unreal projects (each has its own licence: check).
- **Make our own in Blender.** Most geological forms are rules, not hand-sculpting. Basalt columns are Voronoi cells extruded. Stacked slabs and layered towers are noisy discs piled up. Spires are tapered, fractured extrusions. Pillow lava is merged blobs. A Blender script can produce a whole formation family, each piece different, deterministic, and ours, with no licence question. It's how the seabed and the walkable shell are already made. Scanned Megascans materials can then texture them.

Recommendation: Megascans (with licences checked) for **surfaces and small debris**, where scans beat anything procedural, and our own Blender generators for **big formations**, where the shape matters more than the scan detail and must follow the region's geology.

## Claude's notes: making it better

These go beyond the reference. Take them or leave them.

- **Let the sonar read the geology.** Soft sediment absorbs sound and rock reflects it. If each surface material has an acoustic hardness, the sonar can show substrate as well as depth: a plain shows as soft, dim returns, and a bedrock shelf lights up. Regions then become recognisable on the chart before anyone sees them, which fits "recognise regions by their geology alone" and gives the sonar station real work.
- **Reveal big things by sound, small things by light.** The floodlights reach about 100 m, so the monumental scale can never be seen. It can be heard and charted. The best version of the owner's canyon moment: the sonar paints a wall rising 400 m on the chart, then the searchlights find only the first ledge. The fog and the light range are tuned so that the eyes see the detail and the sonar sees the scale.
- **Currents tie the world together.** One current direction per region can drive ripple orientation, which side sediment piles up on, the marine snow's drift and the shape of plumes. It's cheap, and it makes everything feel caused.
- **Empty on purpose, so the sonar is quiet too.** The Silent Plains should be acoustically empty as well: a flat chart and no echoes. Then the single 300 m pillar arrives first as one bright return on an empty chart.
- **Landmarks mirror the ship.** The ship's interior is meant to be easy to get lost in at first. The ocean can work the same way: a few unmistakable silhouettes (the bent pillar, a twin arch, a guyot) become the crew's shared reference points. "Bear 040 from the Twins."
- **Sediment clouds are gameplay.** Flying low or touching bottom should stir up a cloud that blinds the cameras and the window for a while. It gives a reason to hold altitude, and a vivid reaction to the seabed collision that already exists.
- **Start with the cheapest high-impact pieces.** One empty plain and one enormous formation make a better first test than a little of everything.

6. **Sediment clouds** when the hull touches or skims the seabed.
7. **Sonar substrate and finer local resolution.**
8. **Regions and World Partition,** once one region looks right.

## Tuning the look

The water's look is the constants at the top of `create_test_map.py`. Change them, rebuild the map, and capture frames:

```
python Scripts/build/capture_screenshots.py <folder> outside=160/5@17000,-3000,100 bridge=0/-10@13700,0,120
```

A view is `yaw/pitch@X,Y,Z` in ship coordinates. A spot outside the hull puts you in the water.
